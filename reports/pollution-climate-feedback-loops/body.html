Overview

Pollution and climate change are not two problems with a shared source. They are a set of loops that feed each other — and loops can be broken.

The usual account of pollution and climate change is that both come out of the same chimneys and exhausts, so cleaning up one tends to help the other. That is true, but it understates the problem. The two also drive each other. Warming dries forests and lengthens fire seasons, and the fires release carbon and smoke. Warming speeds up the chemistry that makes ground-level ozone and pushes wetlands to release more methane, and both ozone and methane warm the planet further while ozone damages crops and forests. Poverty keeps billions of people cooking with wood and dung, and the smoke makes them ill and keeps them poor. These are feedback loops, and loops behave differently from single problems.

This dashboard maps five of them across health, ecosystems and development and measures their links with public data. Three are closed reinforcing loops; two are amplifier chains, in which climate change magnifies the harm of pollution without closing the circle. The central argument is about how to break them. In a reinforcing loop, the strength of the whole loop is the product of the strengths of its links, so weakening any one link weakens the whole. That means a loop can be broken at its cheapest link — and in several loops the cheapest link is very cheap indeed.

The argument in brief

Pollution and climate change are joined by reinforcing loops that run through forests, wetlands, crops, oceans and households. In each loop, the loop's strength is the product of its links, so cutting any link by half halves the loop. That changes the strategy: instead of attacking the largest link, which is usually global emissions, a loop can be broken at its cheapest link — leaking gas wells, local fire management, cooking stoves — while the larger work continues. The best entry points cut several loops at once.

The evidence assembled here is deliberately conservative. Where a link is well measured — methane concentrations, burned area, PM2.5 exposure, cooking fuels — the dashboard shows the data. Where only research estimates exist — the doubling of extreme fires, ozone's damage to crops, the synergy between heat and particles — it cites them and their uncertainty. Where a dataset carries its own warning, as the Global Fire Emissions Database does for its recent years, the warning is repeated. The loop-gain calculator in section 7 uses illustrative strengths; its purpose is to show the algebra of breaking loops, not to estimate any particular loop.

Section 1 maps the five loops and lets readers explore them one at a time. Sections 2 to 6 take each in turn with its data. Section 7 sets out the algebra of breaking loops, an interactive calculator and a table of breakers — the cheapest known way to weaken each loop, and what it buys. The method section lists every source and assumption.

1. Five Loops

Five pathways connect pollution and climate to health, ecosystems and development. Choose one to trace it.

The map has five nodes. Climate and pollution sit at the centre-line; health, ecosystems and development sit around them, because that is where the damage lands. Each arrow is a causal link with published evidence behind it. Not every pathway closes: some run from climate through pollution into health and stop there, which makes them amplifiers rather than loops. The distinction matters for strategy, because an amplifier can only be damped at its links, while a loop can be broken.

The fire loop and the methane–ozone loop both close through the climate: warming makes more fire and more methane and ozone, and those warm the planet. The cooking loop closes through development: poverty sustains polluting fuels, and the illness they cause sustains poverty. Heat and air is an amplifier: heat makes pollution more harmful and more abundant. Ocean deoxygenation is an amplifier where warming and nutrient pollution combine.

What all five share is that each crosses at least two of the three domains in the title. That is why treating pollution and climate separately misses them: a pollution agency sees the smoke, a climate agency the carbon, a health ministry the deaths and an agriculture ministry the lost harvest, and none of them sees the loop. A loop is visible only when the links are drawn together, as here.

2. The Fire Loop

Fewer fires, fiercer fires. Global burned area is falling while the most extreme fires multiply and release carbon at national scale.

The fire loop is the clearest case of climate and pollution feeding each other. Hotter, drier conditions lengthen fire seasons and dry out fuels; extreme fires release carbon that adds to warming and smoke that reaches hundreds of millions of people. Satellite records show the shape of the change. Global burned area has actually fallen — by about a fifth between 2012 and 2024 in the Global Wildfire Information System data, mainly in savannas and grasslands, a decline researchers have linked largely to the conversion of grassland to farmland — but the most intense fires have multiplied. Cunningham and colleagues found that extreme wildfires became 2.2 times more frequent between 2003 and 2023, and 7.3 times more frequent in boreal forests.

The carbon scale of those extremes is national. The Global Fire Emissions Database puts the carbon released by fires in boreal North America in 2023 at about 769 million tonnes, roughly twelve times its 1997–2022 average; GFED flags its post-2017 numbers as preliminary, but an independent top-down estimate by Byrne and colleagues puts Canada's 2023 fires at about 647 million tonnes of carbon, more than the annual fossil emissions of all but three countries. Smoke from landscape fires is associated with about 1.5 million deaths a year worldwide, more than 90 per cent of them in low- and middle-income countries.

Most burned area is in savannas and grasslands, where fire is declining. The rise in extreme forest fires is hidden in this total — which is why burned area alone understates the loop.

The two charts tell opposite stories, and both are true. Burned area is dominated by fires in African and South American savannas, which have declined as grasslands are converted to cropland and pasture. The fires that close the loop are different: forest fires in boreal and temperate conifer regions, which release far more carbon per hectare, burn through peat and soils, and produce the smoke plumes that cross continents. Those are the fires that have multiplied, and the boreal series shows how far outside the earlier record 2023 fell.

The loop's health link is the most immediate. Landscape fire smoke contributed to about 1.53 million deaths a year on average over 2000–2019, according to Xu and colleagues in The Lancet, mostly through fine particles and partly through ozone. The Lancet Countdown estimated about 154,000 deaths from fire smoke in 2024 alone using a narrower method; the two figures measure different things and should not be compared directly, but both place fire smoke among the larger air-pollution burdens.

The loop's largest link — global warming — can only be weakened slowly, by cutting emissions everywhere. Its other links are local and faster to act on: fuel management and prescribed burning in fire-prone forests, rapid detection and suppression of new ignitions, protection of peatlands, and, at the health end, smoke forecasts, clean-air shelters and filtration. Section 7 returns to why that local work matters even though it does not touch the global driver.

3. Methane and Ozone

Warming wetlands release methane; methane makes ozone; ozone damages the crops and forests that absorb carbon. The cheapest link is a leaking pipe.

Methane is both a greenhouse gas and a precursor of ground-level ozone, which is itself a greenhouse gas and a damaging air pollutant. The atmospheric concentration measured by NOAA has risen from about 1,645 parts per billion in 1984 to about 1,936 in 2025. Its growth slowed in the early 2000s, resumed in 2007 and reached a record 17.7 parts per billion in 2021, with 2020 close behind. Studies of that surge attribute much of it to wetlands, especially in the tropics, emitting more methane as they warmed and grew wetter — the climate feeding the pollutant.

The ozone half of the loop runs through ecosystems. Ozone enters leaves and reduces photosynthesis. Mills and colleagues estimated that ozone reduces global yields of soybean by 12.4 per cent, wheat by 7.1 per cent, maize by 6.1 per cent and rice by 4.4 per cent — about 227 million tonnes of lost harvest a year. The same damage to forests weakens the land carbon sink, so more of each year's emissions stay in the atmosphere.

The methane–ozone loop has an unusually cheap link. Wetland emissions cannot be switched off, but human-caused methane — from oil and gas, coal, landfills and agriculture — can be cut quickly, and because methane lasts only about a decade in the atmosphere, cuts show up in temperature and ozone within years rather than decades. The UNEP and Climate and Clean Air Coalition Global Methane Assessment found that cutting human-caused methane by 45 per cent this decade would avoid nearly 0.3 °C of warming by 2045, about 260,000 premature deaths, 775,000 asthma-related hospital visits, 73 billion hours of lost labour and 25 million tonnes of crop losses every year.

The cheapest part of that cut is in fossil fuels. The IEA's Global Methane Tracker estimates that fossil-fuel operations emitted about 124 million tonnes of methane in 2025, that about 70 per cent could be abated with existing technology, and that about 30 per cent — more than 35 million tonnes a year — could be abated at no net cost, because the captured gas is worth more than the equipment to capture it. Few climate measures pay for themselves; fixing leaks, ending routine flaring and venting, and capturing coal-mine methane do.

The growth of methane slowed in 2023–2025, to 4.8 parts per billion in 2025, the lowest in nearly two decades. Whether that reflects cooler, drier conditions in tropical wetlands, changes in the atmosphere's capacity to destroy methane, or early effects of abatement is not yet settled. The loop remains: as the climate warms, wetland emissions are expected to rise, which makes the human-caused share the part worth cutting now.

4. Heat and Air

Heat makes pollution more abundant and more deadly. This is an amplifier, not a loop — and the damage concentrates where exposure is already highest.

Warming changes the chemistry of polluted air. Higher temperatures and stronger sunlight speed up the reactions that form ozone, and heatwaves often come with stagnant air that traps particles. The IPCC's assessment, as summarised by the World Meteorological Organization, is that with about 3 °C of warming, surface ozone would rise by about 20 per cent over Pakistan, northern India and Bangladesh and about 10 per cent over eastern China — regions that are home to roughly a quarter of the world's population. Climate change, in other words, raises the air-pollution burden for the same emissions: a "climate penalty".

Heat and pollution also compound in the body. A 2025 study of about 3.6 million deaths in ten Indian cities found that the mortality risk associated with each 10 µg/m³ of PM2.5 rose from about 0.8 per cent on warm days (the 75th temperature percentile) to about 4.6 per cent on the hottest days (the 99th). Studies in the United States and Portugal report similar synergies. Hot, polluted days are more dangerous than the sum of their parts.

Exposure has turned a corner, but not everywhere. Population-weighted PM2.5 exposure worldwide rose from about 24 µg/m³ in 1998 to a peak of about 35 in 2016, then fell to about 30 by 2024, in large part reflecting China's clean-air programme. In high-income countries it fell from about 16 to 13. In lower-middle-income countries — including India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nigeria — it fell from its 2016 peak of about 51 to about 43. In low-income countries it rose over the period, from about 23 to 30. Every group remains far above the WHO guideline of 5.

Because the heat amplifier works on existing pollution, its harm concentrates where both are high: the Indo-Gangetic plain, the Sahel and the megacities of South and West Asia. These places are also where heat is rising fastest relative to people's ability to cool themselves. The amplifier cannot be broken, because heat will keep rising for decades; it can be damped from both ends — by cutting the precursors that heat turns into pollution, and by protecting people on the days when heat and pollution coincide.

The protective end is proven. Ahmedabad's Heat Action Plan, India's first, combined early warnings, public cooling and health-system preparation; an evaluation associated it with about 1,190 avoided deaths a year (95 per cent confidence interval 162 to 2,218). Pairing heat warnings with air-quality alerts — and treating hot, polluted days as a single emergency — is a low-cost way to damp the amplifier while the precursors are cut.

5. Oceans

Warming takes oxygen out of the ocean from above; nutrient pollution takes it out of the coasts from the land. Fisheries are caught between.

The ocean is losing oxygen. Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen and mixes less with the deep ocean, and the open ocean has lost about 2 per cent of its oxygen since the mid-twentieth century, according to the studies summarised by Breitburg and colleagues in Science. Some regions have lost far more. This is the climate half of the amplifier: warming on its own lowers oxygen everywhere.

The pollution half is concentrated on coasts. Fertiliser and sewage running off the land feed algal blooms that, as they decay, strip oxygen from coastal waters. Reported coastal dead zones have risen from fewer than 50 in 1950 to more than 500, and monitoring is patchy enough that the true number is likely higher. Where warming and nutrient pollution combine, low-oxygen zones expand, last longer and push fish and shellfish out of their habitats.

The development consequence falls on the coastal communities that depend on those fisheries. The amplifier is damped most directly at the pollution end: more efficient fertiliser use, wetland buffers on farmland, and wastewater treatment in coastal cities. These measures are local, well understood and pay off within years — which, as in the other loops, makes them the cheapest place to start while the global driver is addressed.

6. The Cooking Loop

Poverty keeps 2.1 billion people cooking with smoke; the smoke keeps them poor. The loop is closing in Asia and widening in sub-Saharan Africa.

The cooking loop runs entirely through development. Households that cannot afford gas, electricity or modern stoves cook with wood, charcoal, crop waste, dung or kerosene. The smoke is among the largest single sources of air-pollution deaths: WHO's latest estimate is about 2.9 million a year, and the IEA's is higher. Illness, lost school time and the hours spent collecting fuel — overwhelmingly by women and girls — keep those households poor, which keeps them on polluting fuels. Fuelwood and charcoal demand also degrades forests and releases black carbon, linking the loop to ecosystems and climate.

The loop has been weakening globally. The share of humanity without access to clean cooking fuels fell from 53 per cent in 2000 to about 25 per cent in 2024; China, India and Indonesia each halved the number without access after 2010, largely through free stoves and subsidised liquefied petroleum gas. But in sub-Saharan Africa the number lacking access is still rising, by about 14 million people a year, because population is growing faster than access.

The cooking loop's cheapest link is the fuel. The IEA estimates that universal access to clean cooking by 2030 would require about $8 billion of investment a year — a very small sum against the 2.9 to 3.7 million deaths a year that household smoke causes, and against the global energy system's annual investment. The same transition would reduce greenhouse gas emissions by about 1.5 billion tonnes a year, mostly by reducing unsustainable fuelwood harvesting and the methane and black carbon from incomplete combustion.

Breaking the loop at the fuel does more than remove smoke. It frees time for school and paid work, reduces pressure on forests and cuts emissions of short-lived warmers, so it weakens the development loop and touches the fire and climate loops at the same time. This is the pattern this dashboard is built to show: the best interventions cut several loops at once.

The obstacle is affordability, not technology. Households switch when clean fuels are cheaper and more reliable than the alternatives, which is why the successful national programmes subsidised stoves and refills and expanded distribution. Sub-Saharan Africa, where the number without access is still growing, is where the next $8 billion a year would do most.

7. Breaking Loops

In a reinforcing loop, every link carries the whole loop. Cut the cheapest one first.

A reinforcing loop amplifies any push it receives. If the loop's links have strengths s₁, s₂ and s₃ — how much a change in one quantity moves the next — the loop gain is their product, G = s₁ × s₂ × s₃, and any external push is amplified by a factor of 1 / (1 − G). A loop with a gain of one half doubles every push. The useful consequence is that the gain depends on every link equally: halving any one link halves the gain, whichever link it is. In the illustrative loop below, that removes two-thirds of the amplification.

That turns strategy around. The largest link in most of these loops is global warming, which can only be weakened slowly and collectively. But the loop can be weakened just as much, in proportional terms, by cutting a smaller link that is cheap and local — a leaking gas field, a fire-prone forest, a household stove. Two of the breakers stand out because they touch several loops at once. Cutting methane weakens the methane–ozone loop, reduces the ozone that heat turns into a health emergency, and protects crops and forests. Clean cooking weakens the development loop, reduces pressure on forests and fire, and cuts short-lived warmers. These are the interventions to fund first, because each dollar works in more than one loop — and in both cases the cost per unit of harm avoided is among the lowest known.

One loop works against the others. Sulphur dioxide from coal and shipping is a deadly air pollutant, but the aerosols it forms also cool the planet by reflecting sunlight. Cleaning it up — rightly — removes some of that cooling, as this series examined in One Source, Two Signs. The answer is not to keep the sulphur but to pair clean-air policy with fast cuts in methane and other short-lived warmers, so that the health gains of clean air are not paid for in extra warming.

Breaking loops at their cheapest links does not replace cutting carbon dioxide; it buys time and health while that slower work proceeds. Every loop in this dashboard is driven in the end by warming, and every one gets harder to break as warming continues. The practical message is about sequence: act first where the cost is lowest and the benefit spans the most loops, then keep going on the links that cost more.
