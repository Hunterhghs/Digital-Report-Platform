The reading

For most of the period since 2000, global stresses arrived one or two at a time: a price spike here, a financial panic there. In the 2020s they began to arrive together — five indicators at once in 2021, all six in 2022 — even though, year to year, the underlying systems rarely move in step. That combination, independent systems in simultaneous distress on a rising chronic floor, is what the word polycrisis is for.

3. Independent, Then Together

If one underlying driver moved all six indicators, their simultaneous distress would be unremarkable. It doesn't: most pairs are nearly uncorrelated.

Whichever threshold is used, the 2020s show at least twice — and up to roughly seven times — the co-occurrence of either earlier decade. The result does not depend on where the line is drawn.

Why this matters

Risk management that treats each system separately — a food reserve here, a financial backstop there — is calibrated to a world in which shocks arrive one at a time. Low correlations mean that assumption is usually right. But buffers sized for one shock are drawn down together when several coincide, and the 2020s are the period in which that coincidence stopped being rare.

5. Anatomy of Systemic Risk

How independent shocks become a single crisis: the mechanism the data above can see, and the parts it cannot.

Definition

The Cascade Institute defines a global polycrisis as one in which crises in multiple global systems become causally entangled in ways that significantly degrade humanity's prospects, producing harms greater than the crises would produce in isolation. Systemic risk is the underlying condition that makes this possible: a small problem can spread quickly through a system and into others.

What the dashboard can and cannot show

It shows co-occurrence: how many systems are under acute stress in the same year, and how far that departs from their usual independence. It does not observe entanglement — the causal channels by which one crisis worsens another. High breadth is necessary for a polycrisis, not proof of one.

6. Method

How the stress-breadth index is built, and the choices that could change it.

The index counts, for each year, how many of six acute indicators fall in their own worst quartile over 2000–2025. Thresholds are computed per indicator with linear interpolation; the calendar also lets readers use the worst third or the worst fifth. Food and oil are measured as year-on-year price changes rather than levels, because both trend upward over the period. Market volatility, policy uncertainty, battle deaths and disaster events are measured as annual levels.

Three choices deserve scrutiny. Equal weighting treats a disaster year and a financial panic as equivalent units of stress, which they are not; the index measures breadth, not severity. Disaster counts reflect improving reporting as well as underlying change, which tends to understate the early 2000s. And the 2000–2025 window is short enough that a decade of unusual calm, such as the 2010s, lowers every threshold. The finding that the 2020s are exceptional holds at all three thresholds tested.
