Executive Summary

The bottleneck in resilience is no longer the invention. It is the decision to use it.

FINDING 01

Good ideas travel slowly — and the reason is cost, not ignorance

Adoption is a decision, and every decision has costs: money up front, time to learn, nerve to trust, effort to search. Those costs, not a lack of information, are why proven resilience tools can take decades to spread.

FINDING 02

A polycrisis raises the value of protection and the price of adopting it

Shocks make resilience worth more — and simultaneously destroy the savings, credit and confidence people need to buy it. The people who most need protection are the ones least able to pay for it in the moment it matters.

FINDING 03

Adoption pays everyone, so the market under-buys it

One person's adoption protects the neighbours, the supply chain and the public budget. Because that benefit leaks, private choices are too timid — which is exactly where public money and smart design earn their keep.

This report is about one question: why do some life-saving ideas spread like wildfire while others, just as good, sit on the shelf for decades? The answer is economics, and it matters more now than ever. We live in a polycrisis — a time when climate, health, financial and political shocks arrive on several fronts at once. In that world, resilience is not a luxury. It is the difference between a bad year and a lost decade.

The encouraging part is that we already know most of what we need. The technologies of resilience exist: cheaper solar, better seeds, early warning systems, mobile money, index insurance. The hard part has never been inventing them. It has been getting millions of ordinary people — and the institutions around them — to adopt them. That is a problem of price, risk, trust and time. It is solvable, and this report explains how.

1. The Puzzle of the Slow Good Idea

The tools of resilience are cheap, proven and available. So why do so many people skip them?

Start with a puzzle. A drought-tolerant seed costs about the same as an ordinary seed and can save a harvest in a dry year. A reflective roof coating costs a few hundred dollars and can cut a home's indoor heat dramatically. An SMS warning can reach a farmer before a storm destroys a season's work. None of these is exotic. All of them have been proven in the field. And all of them are adopted far more slowly than the numbers say they should be.

The standard answer is information: people don't know the tools exist, or don't understand them. That is part of the story, but it is the smallest part. An earlier report in this series, Scaling Low-Cost Adaptation Technologies, showed that two technologies delivered through the same channel, at the same price, to the same households can spread at wildly different speeds. The difference was not information. It was the design of the product and the structure of the decision.

Economists think about adoption as a decision under uncertainty. A household is not asked whether a technology is good in general. It is asked whether the technology is worth it for them, right now, given the money they have, the risks they carry and the examples they can see around them. That question has a shape, and the shape matters.

Figure 1 — The shape of adoption

Schematic, not data. Adoption almost always follows an S-curve: slow while trust is scarce and costs are high, fast once examples and falling costs feed on each other, then flat when the willing adopters are reached. Policy works best when it shortens the slow start rather than pushing the plateau.

Adoption is also getting faster, which tells us the slow start can be compressed. The telephone needed about 75 years to reach 50 million users. Radio needed 38. Television needed 13. Mobile phones needed about 12, and the internet needed four. Each generation borrowed the trust and the rails of the one before it. The same compression is available to resilience tools if we build on what people already use. Two companion reports in this series take the wider view: S-Curves Against the Clock races technology curves against the carbon budget, and The Architecture of Global Technological Diffusion measures them country by country. This report stays close to the individual decision.

Figure 2 — Each wave adopts faster than the last

Approximate years to reach 50 million users. Figures vary by source because "user" means different things across eras; the direction is the point. A technology that rides on rails people already trust can skip decades of slow start.

2. What Adoption Costs

Five costs, in plain language, decide whether a good idea spreads. Almost every adoption programme fails on one of them.

When an economist says adoption is "costly", they do not mean only money. They mean everything a person gives up or risks by trying something new. Break the decision open and five costs appear. Each one can stop a good idea on its own.

Table 1 — The five costs of adopting

Cost In plain language Who feels it most
Upfront money The price, paid before any benefit arrives — a pump, a panel, a premium. Households with irregular incomes and no savings.
Learning Time and effort to understand the thing and change how you work. Busy people whose routines already work well enough.
Risk The chance it fails, breaks or underperforms — with no refund. Anyone with no cushion to absorb a bad bet.
Search Finding out the thing exists and whether to believe it. People far from markets, networks and trusted advice.
Coordination Needing others to move first — a warning system, a shared canal, a grid. Anyone whose benefit depends on neighbours or government.

None of these is exotic. Together they explain more adoption failure than any amount of "people just don't understand."

Read the table and a strategy almost writes itself. If the upfront money is the barrier, break the payment into small pieces. If learning is the barrier, make the first experience free and easy. If risk is the barrier, guarantee the result or refund the failure. If search is the barrier, put the product where people already look, with a face they already trust. If coordination is the barrier, recognise that this is not a product problem at all — it is a public goods problem, and no marketing campaign will fix it.

The point is that each cost has a known counter-move. The previous report in this series mapped those moves onto real technologies: pay-as-you-go solar fixed the upfront-money problem by collecting tiny instalments over mobile money, and it exploded across Africa and South Asia. The stove that is sold on "faster cooking tonight" beats the stove sold on "less lung disease in twenty years", because the first pitch targets the cost people feel and the second targets a benefit nobody can see.

3. What a Polycrisis Changes

Shocks make protection worth more — and harder to buy. That collision is the core of the problem.

A polycrisis is not one big emergency. It is many emergencies arriving at once: a heatwave in the same season as a price spike, a flood in the same year as a debt crisis, a pandemic on top of a drought. The data shows the pile-up. The United States recorded 27 weather and climate disasters costing a billion dollars or more in 2024 alone — the second-highest count on record, behind only 2023. The 2020–2024 average was 23 such events a year, against a 1980–2024 average of 9. The baseline is moving, and it is moving fast.

Figure 3 — The baseline is moving

US billion-dollar weather and climate disasters, per NOAA NCEI. The recent five-year average is more than double the historical average, and 2024 came within one event of the record. A polycrisis is this curve layered on top of itself across climate, health and finance.

Here is the collision. A shock does two things to the adoption decision at once. It raises the value of protection: after a flood, a raised foundation or an early warning subscription is worth dramatically more than it was the week before. But the same shock also destroys savings, tightens credit and makes people more cautious about every gamble — including the good ones. The household that most needs the pump is the household whose cash was just washed away.

Timing makes it worse. The moment when people are most willing to pay for protection is immediately after a disaster. The moment when they have the least ability to pay is also immediately after a disaster. Six months later the ability has partly recovered but the urgency has faded. The classic result is a wave of interest after each disaster, followed by a quiet slide back to old habits — until the next one. Policy that waits for disasters to motivate adoption is always late.

The protection paradox

Resilience is cheapest to buy before the shock and easiest to justify after it. The gap between those two moments is where adoption dies. The fix is to move the decision before the disaster — through pre-approved finance, automatic enrolment and standing offers that activate when the risk rises, not after the loss.

4. The Curve That Pays You Back

The more people adopt, the cheaper the thing gets. Solar proved it. Every resilience technology can copy it.

The single most hopeful fact in this field is that adoption feeds on itself. When many people buy a technology, producers learn to make it better and cheaper, suppliers compete, installers get experienced, and the next buyer pays less. Economists call it learning-by-doing; engineers call it the learning curve. Solar power is the poster child.

In 2010, utility-scale solar electricity cost about $0.46 per kilowatt-hour — far more expensive than fossil power. By 2023 the global average was $0.044, a fall of about 90 per cent. The total installed cost per kilowatt fell 86 per cent over the same period. And here is the part that matters for this report: solar did not get cheap first and spread later. It spread first — on subsidies, mandates and early enthusiasm — and then it got cheap. Adoption caused the cost decline at least as much as the cost decline caused adoption.

Figure 4 — Adoption made solar cheap

Global weighted-average levelised cost of electricity for utility-scale solar PV, per IRENA's Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2023. The 90 per cent fall was earned by the adopters — which is exactly the deal on offer for every resilience technology we are slow to buy.

Figure 5 — The deal changed sides

Solar's global levelised cost relative to the weighted-average fossil alternative, per IRENA. In 2010 solar was 414 per cent more expensive; by 2023 it was 56 per cent cheaper. That flip is what a learning curve buys.

The implication is a bargain, not a cost. Every subsidy that puts a resilience technology into more hands does two jobs at once: it protects the person who receives it, and it pushes the technology down its learning curve for everyone who comes after. The money spent on early adoption is not consumption. It is investment in a cheaper future — the same logic that built the solar miracle.

The catch is that the bargain only works if the adopters keep coming. A technology that stalls at the pilot stage never descends its curve. It stays expensive forever, and then everyone concludes it was never viable — a self-fulfilling verdict. The slow start of the S-curve is not an inconvenience. It is the most expensive part of the whole curve, and it is where public money has the highest return.

5. When Good Ideas Stay Private

One person's adoption protects everyone. That is good news for the community and bad news for the market.

Here is a subtlety that decides a lot of policy. When a farmer adopts a drought-tolerant seed, the harvest that survives helps the farmer — and it also helps the farm workers, the grain buyers, the mill and the village that does not have to support a failed household. When one household buys flood insurance, the whole community faces less pressure on its shared resources after a disaster. When one clinic stocks a treatment, the neighbours are safer from the disease. The benefit of adoption leaks outward.

Economists call this a spillover, and it has a precise consequence: people will adopt less than the community would want them to. The adopter pays the full cost but captures only part of the benefit, so the private decision is too timid. It is the same logic as vaccination, where one person's shot protects the unvaccinated around them. Left to itself, the market under-provides protection — not because anyone is foolish, but because the price the adopter pays does not include the value they create for everyone else.

What the spillover means in practice

When a technology's benefit leaks outward, the gap between private and public value is a bill the community should be willing to pay. Subsidies for adoption are not charity. They are the public buying, at a discount, a benefit it would otherwise receive for free and by luck. The same logic applies to early warning: nobody can sell a warning to one household at a time, because a warning only works if everyone receives it. That good must be provided, not marketed.

Two quieter barriers ride along with the spillover. The first is trust. Most people do not adopt a technology because a brochure says it works. They adopt because someone they know tried it and is still using it. That is why adoption is slow at the start and fast in the middle: the S-curve is a trust curve. The second is patience. A poor household is not irrational for choosing food today over protection next year. When the present is urgent, the future is expensive. Discounting the future more steeply than a rich household is not a mistake; it is arithmetic.

Put the three together — spillovers, trust and patience — and the policy conclusion is clear. Adoption problems are not solved by information campaigns. They are solved by changing the deal: lowering the price the adopter pays, guaranteeing the risk they fear, and borrowing the trust they already place in something else.

6. Making Resilience Cheap to Adopt

Four moves, in the order in which they are cheap.

6.1 Break the upfront cost

  • Convert lump sums into instalments that match irregular incomes — the pay-as-you-go solar model, applied to pumps, roofs, stoves and insurance premiums.
  • Pre-approve finance before the disaster, so the money is waiting when the risk rises, not missing when the loss lands.

6.2 Guarantee the risk

  • Refund or replace when the technology fails to perform; a guarantee converts a gamble into a trial.
  • Subsidise the first cycle, not every cycle — the goal is to buy the learning curve, not to buy the product forever.

6.3 Shorten the search

  • Sell the visible benefit first — the cooler room, the faster cooking, the bigger harvest — and bank the invisible one, like averted illness or avoided loss.
  • Recruit the already-trusted: extension agents, local shops, religious and community leaders, and the neighbour whose field or roof is the demonstration.

6.4 Pay for the spillover

  • Treat public subsidies as the purchase of a public benefit, and measure what they buy: sustained use, not units distributed.
  • Provide what cannot be marketed. Early warning, flood defence and hydrometeorological services are public goods; fund them as such and measure coverage.

6.5 Conclusion

The economics of innovation adoption is, at bottom, a theory of one decision: a person, a price, a risk and a neighbour. Everything we know about that decision says the same thing. Adoption fails on cost, risk, search and trust — and it succeeds when those are lowered, guaranteed, shortened and borrowed. In a polycrisis, the cost of getting this wrong is not a slower rollout. It is a disaster that arrives on top of a disaster, in a community that had the tools to soften both and never used them.

The tools exist. The evidence is in. The only thing left is the decision — and the decision can be bought, with better design, cheaper finance and the public money that the spillover justifies. The binding term is adoption, not invention.
