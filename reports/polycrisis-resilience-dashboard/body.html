The reading

Every pressure indicator on this dashboard is higher than it was in 2015. Most capacity indicators have moved little, and the gap between the countries most and least able to adapt is wider than it was in 1995. The exceptions — early warning coverage more than doubled, social protection now reaching over half the world — are both cheap, institution-led measures, which is the most practically useful pattern in the data.

Countries improving since 2015

Share whose 2024 score exceeds 2015 · by income group

Lower-middle-income countries improved most often — 31 of 46. Fewer than half of low-income countries did.

What the gap is made of

ND-GAIN components

Vulnerability combines exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity across six sectors: food, water, health, ecosystem services, human habitat and infrastructure.

Readiness measures the ability to use investment for adaptation: economic, governance and social components.

The score is readiness minus vulnerability, rescaled. A country can improve by reducing exposure, by building institutions, or both — and the data show the second moving more slowly almost everywhere.

5. Where Capacity Grew

The clearest gains in the data were built deliberately, at scale, and cheaply.

Countries with multi-hazard early warning systems

Number of countries reporting · UNDRR & WMO

From 52 in 2015 to 119 in 2025 — 60 per cent of countries. Coverage remains lowest in small island states (43%) and least-developed countries.

Global social protection coverage

% of world population with at least one cash benefit · ILO

Over half the world is now covered for the first time — but the gain is concentrated in middle-income countries.

Why these two moved

Early warning and social protection share three properties. Their unit cost is low relative to the losses they avoid. They are built by institutions — meteorological services, social registries, payment systems — rather than by large capital projects. And they work against every hazard rather than one, which is what matters when shocks converge. They are the closest thing in this dataset to a general-purpose resilience investment.

6. Reading the Dashboard

What the indicators can and cannot tell you.

This dashboard places indicators from different sources, years and methods side by side. It does not combine them into a composite "polycrisis resilience score", because doing so would require weighting judgements that the evidence cannot settle and would hide the variation that makes the comparison useful.

The central measure of adaptive capacity is the ND-GAIN Country Index, which is designed for climate adaptation rather than for every kind of shock. It is used here because it is the most complete long-run, country-level measure of the combination that matters — exposure to hazards and the institutional and economic readiness to respond — and because its readiness component captures much of what resilience to non-climate shocks also depends on. Readers should treat it as a strong proxy, not a complete measure.

Three cautions apply. Income groups use current World Bank classifications across the whole series, so countries that changed group are counted in their present one. The quadrant lines are global medians, a reference rather than a threshold with policy meaning. And several pressure indicators — disasters, displacement — reflect reporting coverage as well as underlying change, which tends to understate the earlier years.

Related analysis in this series develops the argument behind several panels: financing the synergy on the vulnerability premium in sovereign borrowing; the last third on why recurring public services stalled; and the economics of prevention on why early warning outperforms response spending.
