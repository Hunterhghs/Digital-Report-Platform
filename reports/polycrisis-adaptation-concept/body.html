Overview

Modern societies rarely face one disruption at a time. A drought can weaken agricultural output, strain hydroelectric grids, raise food inflation, tighten budgets and amplify social or geopolitical instability. Polycrisis Adaptation is the H Heuristics concept for adjusting to that condition on purpose, across systems, rather than one crisis at a time.

Definition · a concept developed by Hunter Hughes, Founder of H Heuristics

Polycrisis Adaptation

The process through which economic, social, institutional, technological, and infrastructural systems adjust to interconnected and evolving global risks, strengthening their capacity to manage disruptions and changing conditions.

Central thesis

Adaptation should not be treated as a collection of isolated responses to individual crises; it must become an integrated development process accounting for interactions across environmental, economic, technological, demographic, and geopolitical risks.

The idea in brief

From fixing to adjusting. Most public policy and development planning still treats adaptation as a series of isolated, reactive fixes: a seawall here, a supply-chain patch there, emergency relief after the shock. Polycrisis Adaptation replaces them with a unified development discipline that anticipates cross-domain spillovers before they compound.

Payoffs counted across risks. A decentralised solar-plus-storage microgrid buffers against extreme-weather outages, reduces imported fuel exposure, stabilises local clinics and supports digital connectivity. Evaluated for one hazard it looks like a cost; evaluated for its multi-risk payoff it looks like an engine of competitiveness and human security.

A process, not a state. Polycrisis Resilience is the overarching goal: reducing interconnected vulnerabilities and withstanding shocks. Polycrisis Adaptation is the ongoing process by which institutions, markets, networks and communities reconfigure themselves as risks evolve.

1. From Isolated Response to Integrated Adaptation

Siloed adaptation fails for a simple reason: systemic risks interact.

An energy-transition policy that ignores supply-chain concentration creates technological vulnerability. An urban-expansion plan that ignores water stress and demographic ageing builds future fiscal fragility. A seawall protects a coastline and leaves untouched the power grid, the food market and the budget that the same storm will strain. In each case the fix addresses the hazard that was named, and the damage arrives through the system that was not.

Polycrisis Adaptation takes the interaction as its object. It asks how economic, social, institutional, technological and infrastructural systems adjust together to environmental, economic, technological, demographic and geopolitical risks that are linked and changing. Its aim is to strengthen the capacity to manage disruptions and changing conditions, not only to repair the last disruption.

The shift changes the economics of investment. Under conventional budgeting, adaptation spending is often deferred because it is framed as a defensive cost against a single, uncertain hazard. Under Polycrisis Adaptation, investments are evaluated for their multi-risk payoff: the sum of the risks they reduce, across the ministries and systems that would otherwise bear each separately.

Siloed and integrated adaptation compared

A summary of the framework's argument

Adaptation is an established idea; the polycrisis version adds the interaction. The IPCC defines adaptation as the process of adjustment to actual or expected climate and its effects, and disaster-risk practice adds the capacity to resist, absorb, accommodate, adapt to, transform and recover (IPCC 2022; UNDRR 2017). Those definitions are built around hazards. Polycrisis Adaptation extends the same logic to interconnected risks of every kind and to the systems, not only the hazards, that must adjust. A November 2026 OpenAlex title-and-abstract search found one earlier mention of the compound phrase, in a 2025 paper on entrepreneurial resilience, used in a different sense; the definition and framework here are H Heuristics's.

The reactive bias is measurable. Official donors spent 8 cents on disaster prevention and preparedness for each dollar spent on emergency response and reconstruction in developing countries in 2015–2024, and the ratio was 25 to 1 in favour of response in low-income countries (report 2026-74). That is siloed, after-the-fact adaptation in its pure form. The same report assembled 17 published benefit–cost estimates for prevention across ten risk domains: central ratios run from 2 to 87 dollars per dollar and all exceed 1.

INTEGRATED POLYCRISIS ADAPTATION · adjusting to environmental, economic, technological, demographic and geopolitical risks

One system, not five programmes. Strengthening physical assets through infrastructure adaptation requires responsive permitting and cross-agency planning under institutional adaptation. Technological adaptation, from grid-level forecasting to climate-resilient agriculture, only delivers durable security when paired with social adaptation that equips workers and households to navigate economic transitions.

Against single-point dependencies. Economic adaptation ensures that production structures, trade portfolios and fiscal frameworks do not stay locked into fragile single-point dependencies. Diversifying industrial capabilities, building strategic buffers and aligning capital allocation with long-term risk exposure let an economy absorb external shocks without derailing living standards or long-run productivity growth.

The microgrid test. A solar-plus-storage microgrid shows all five at work: infrastructure (the grid), technology (storage and forecasting), economic (less imported-fuel exposure), social (clinics and schools that keep running) and institutional (the permitting and coordination that let it be built). Where one is missing, the others underperform: grid connections in rural Kenya produced few measurable gains in the short run when households lacked appliances, skills and markets (Lee et al. 2020).

3. Compounding Resilience

A defining insight of the framework is that cross-domain investments compound. Siloed responses accumulate hidden vulnerability each time a shock hits.

What the chart says. A system that responds crisis by crisis loses fiscal space and capacity with each shock, so its capacity index drifts from 50 to 35 over twenty years. A system that builds adaptive capacity across domains carries it forward across shock cycles and reaches 110. The gap is the compounding resilience dividend. The chart is the framework's conceptual model with an interpolation slider; it is an illustration of the argument and carries no empirical weight of its own.

What would make it true. The siloed path needs a mechanism for accumulating vulnerability, and the data supply one: shocks to poorer economies leave scars still open when the next arrives (below). The integrated path needs real complementarities, which the series finds present but modest (section 4).

The observed scar. The chart on the right is the one piece of observed evidence on this page. After a severe climate shock (at least 20% of the population affected or damage of at least 5% of GDP), GDP per person in low- and lower-middle-income countries kept falling for four years and was still 3.7% lower after eight. Richer economies show smaller, noisier effects. It is an association, estimated with country and year fixed effects, and it is the cost of low adaptive capacity in the wild.

4. Four Propositions, Tested Against the Series

The framework anchors four research propositions. Here each is set beside the strongest evidence in the H Heuristics series and the literature, with an honest verdict.

Reading the verdicts. Supported: the evidence points the way the proposition says and is reasonably strong. Partly supported: it points that way but is modest, imprecise or indirect. None is a causal test; the propositions are research questions and the evidence is associations and place-based studies.

Where the framework is strongest. Richer, better-governed and better-educated countries suffer far fewer deaths from hazards of similar size (Kahn 2005; Toya and Skidmore 2007; Lutz et al. 2014), and Bangladesh's extreme-weather death rate fell 99% between 1970 and 2007 as preparedness and development advanced together (Haque et al. 2012).

Where it needs work. Cross-domain compounding is real but weak in national data: gains in one dimension go with gains in others (average partial correlation +0.06; strongest links technology–institutions +0.33), and the 2026 series found that risk domains are close to independent in exposure, so benefits in one domain do not automatically show up in another. Compounding depends on design: integrated delivery, not integrated labels.

5. Where It Sits

Polycrisis Adaptation operationalises Polycrisis Resilience in motion and connects the other H Heuristics concepts.

Resilience and adaptation, distinguished. Polycrisis Resilience is defined as the capacity of individuals, communities, institutions, economies, societies and interconnected systems to anticipate, withstand, adapt to, recover from and reduce their vulnerability to multiple interacting crises (Hughes 2026b). Adaptation is one of its verbs and one of its six capacities. Polycrisis Adaptation takes that verb as a subject: it examines the active, ongoing process of adjustment, how institutions, markets, networks and communities continuously reconfigure as risks evolve, where Polycrisis Resilience names the goal of reduced vulnerability.

The wider system. The framework pairs with Polycrisis-Adjusted Development, so that progress is evaluated against systemic risk exposure, and extends Adaptive Development by modelling how environmental, economic, technological, demographic and geopolitical risks interact. It also reinforces Century Pathways and Sustainable Convergence: in a century of rising external hazards, sustaining human-development gains depends on building adaptive capacity directly into infrastructure, institutions and technology diffusion (Hughes 2026a).

6. Three Shifts and a Learning Loop

Where institutions adopt these shifts, adaptation stops being an emergency expense and becomes a durable engine of competitiveness and human security.

Three institutional shifts

What implementing Polycrisis Adaptation requires, with what is already being done

Continuous feedback and learning. Global risk landscapes evolve quickly: climate thresholds shift, supply chains realign and new technologies emerge. Polycrisis Adaptation therefore treats every policy cycle as an opportunity to update standards, reallocate capital toward proven resilience multipliers and strengthen local institutional capacity. Adaptation is a loop, not a plan: assess the risks and their interactions, adjust across the five dimensions, observe what the next shock reveals, and revise.

Honest limits. Adaptation does not remove the need for mitigation or emergency response, and some losses cannot be adapted to. Integration has costs: a poorly designed cross-ministry body can slow everything down. The returns quoted are averages that hide wide variation, and the countries with the thinnest fiscal space (interest exceeds health spending for 2.7 billion people; report 2026-79) are least able to pay up front: the case for blended finance.
