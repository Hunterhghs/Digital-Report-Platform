Overview

Putting a price on the polycrisis is an accounting exercise before it is a forecast. The answer depends on what goes in the ledger.

The polycrisis is usually described in physical terms — degrees of warming, deaths, displaced people, breached boundaries. This dashboard asks the question finance ministers, investors and voters eventually ask: what is it costing in money, and what could it cost by the end of the century? The honest answer is a range, not a number, because the published estimates measure different things. Some count lost output, some count welfare including the value of lives, some count money spent defending against insecurity, and some count damage that this year's emissions will cause decades from now. Adding them all together double-counts; leaving them out understates.

So the dashboard is built as a ledger. Section 1 lists the best published annual estimates for each account and lets readers choose which to include. Section 2 explains the accounting choices that move the total. Section 3 sets out the evidence on how losses grow. Section 4 is a century calculator that turns annual shares of output into a bill for 2025–2100, with growth and discounting under the reader's control. Section 5 asks what prevention would buy. Every figure is sourced, every assumption is visible, and the headline numbers change with the settings.

The bill in brief

Counted narrowly, as lost market output from disasters and heat, the polycrisis costs about 1 per cent of world output a year. Counted as economists value welfare — including the health damage of pollution and the economic impact of violence — it costs 8 to 14 per cent, and more if security spending and the climate damage locked in by each year's emissions are added. Over the century the share is expected to grow. Because world output between 2025 and 2100 will total about $26 quadrillion, even modest shares add up to hundreds of trillions of dollars, and plausible paths reach several quadrillion. Prevention costs a small fraction of that.

The headline in the strapline — about $262 trillion for every percentage point of output lost each year — is the most useful number in the dashboard, because it converts any estimate into a century bill. A polycrisis costing 2 per cent of output a year, a conservative reading of today's market losses plus a share of the health burden, costs about half a quadrillion dollars by 2100. One costing 10 per cent, closer to welfare-based estimates today, costs more than two and a half quadrillion. Neither number is a forecast; both are the arithmetic of a large and growing economy.

The same arithmetic applies to prevention, which is why the dashboard ends there. Spending that avoids even part of the loss is worth a great deal when the loss is measured against a century of world output. Section 5 shows that a prevention effort of 1.5 per cent of output a year, if it avoids 40 per cent of losses, roughly doubles its money — a conservative return compared with published estimates for adaptation and resilience investment.

1. The Annual Ledger

The best published estimate for each account, in its own year. Tick an entry to include it; the total updates as a share of 2025 world output.

The ledger has four accounts. Output entries measure market losses: assets destroyed by disasters and income lost when heat makes work impossible. Welfare entries include the money value of lives and health, as economists conventionally measure them, and the wider economic impact of violence. Response entries measure money spent defending against insecurity — real costs, but spending rather than destruction. Liability entries measure future damage incurred now, chiefly the climate damage that each year's emissions will cause over the following decades, and projected losses not yet realised.

The presets give four standard readings. "Market losses" counts output only. "With health" adds the health cost of air pollution. "With violence" adds the non-military economic impact of violence. "Full ledger" adds world military spending and the climate liability of current emissions. Entries known to overlap others are shaded and off by default, and the climate-attributable extreme-weather estimate is shown for reference because most of it is already inside the disaster and health entries.

The narrowest reading — market losses from disasters and heat — comes to about $1.4 trillion a year, 1.2 per cent of world output. Even this understates market losses, because it omits the output cost of conflict, the productivity cost of pollution-related illness and the disruption that infrastructure failures impose beyond the assets destroyed: the World Bank estimates that infrastructure disruptions alone cost households and firms in low- and middle-income countries hundreds of billions of dollars a year.

Adding the health cost of air pollution raises the total to about $9.5 trillion, 8 per cent of output. This is the largest single entry and the most contested, because it values premature deaths at what people are willing to pay to reduce mortality risk. It is also the entry most directly tied to the energy system: most of it would disappear with clean air, which is why the IMF counts unpriced air pollution as the largest part of the $6.7 trillion of implicit fossil-fuel subsidies it estimated for 2024.

Adding the non-military impact of violence brings the total to about $17 trillion, 14 per cent of output, and the full ledger — with military spending and the climate liability of current emissions — to about $28 trillion, 23.5 per cent. That upper figure mixes present losses, defensive spending and future damage, and should be read as an upper bound rather than a total. The useful conclusion is the range itself: depending on the question asked, the polycrisis costs between one and twenty-odd per cent of world output a year.

2. Why the Number Moves

Six accounting choices explain most of the difference between estimates of the cost of the polycrisis.

Output or welfare. National accounts record what is produced and sold; they do not record a death, except as lost earnings. Welfare measures add the value people place on life and health, which for air pollution is much larger than the lost output. Neither is wrong, but they answer different questions: what the economy loses, and what people lose. Flows or liabilities. A flood destroys assets this year; a tonne of CO2 emitted this year causes damage for decades. Counting the social cost of this year's emissions adds a liability that will not appear in any year's losses until long afterwards.

Market or purchasing-power dollars. The economic impact of violence is published in purchasing-power terms, which value spending in poorer countries more highly. Applied to market output it is smaller in dollars, though the same share. Overlap. The climate-attributable share of extreme weather is already inside disaster losses and health costs; the health cost of pollution overlaps with fossil-fuel subsidies and with the social cost of carbon's health component. A defensible total includes each loss once.

Level or growth. Most estimates treat losses as a share of output in a given year. If a crisis lowers the growth rate rather than the level, the loss compounds: a polycrisis that shaves 0.2 percentage points off growth each year leaves the world economy about 14 per cent smaller by 2100. Discounting. A dollar lost in 2090 counts for less today than a dollar lost now. At a 2 per cent discount rate, the present value of the central century bill is less than two-fifths of its undiscounted total; at 3 per cent, about a quarter. Section 4 lets readers choose.

3. How Losses Grow

Projections agree on direction and disagree on scale. The largest numbers come from climate damage estimates, and one of the most cited has been retracted.

The chart sets current loss estimates beside projections, all as shares of world output. Today's entries come from the ledger. The projections come from institutions that have modelled specific channels: Swiss Re's climate stress test, which found that climate change could cut world output by 11 to 18 per cent by 2050 in higher-warming scenarios; the World Bank's estimates for antimicrobial resistance, 1.1 to 3.8 per cent of output by 2050; and its projection that the collapse of selected ecosystem services would cost about $2.7 trillion a year by 2030, about 2 per cent of output.

The NGFS scenarios used by central banks raised their estimate of chronic climate losses under current policies to as much as 15 per cent of output by 2050 in 2024, roughly tripling the previous figure. That revision rested on a damage function from Kotz and colleagues' 2024 Nature paper, which has since been retracted; the bar is shown hatched and should not be relied on. The range of plausible climate damage remains wide, but it does not depend on that study.

Three patterns stand out. First, today's largest losses are not climate damages but the health cost of pollution and the economic impact of violence, each between 6 and 12 per cent of output on welfare measures. The polycrisis is already expensive before its climate component reaches full scale. Second, climate losses are the fastest-growing entry: disaster losses, heat losses and the stock of damaging emissions all rise with warming, and the higher-warming projections for 2050 are larger than any current entry.

Third, the projections are not additive either. A world in which climate damage reaches 15 per cent of output is one in which health, migration and conflict costs also rise, partly because of the same warming, and the estimates cannot simply be stacked. That is the polycrisis point: interacting losses can exceed the sum of the separately modelled channels, or overlap with them, and no published model captures the interaction well.

For the century calculator, this evidence suggests a range of starting shares between about 2 per cent (a narrow, mostly market measure) and 8 per cent (a welfare measure without violence), rising by 2100 to between 5 and 20 per cent. The presets in section 4 use exactly those bounds, with a central path from 4 to 10 per cent. Readers who think the evidence points elsewhere can move the sliders.

4. The Century Bill

Turn an annual share of output into a bill for 2025–2100. Growth, the path of losses and the discount rate are yours to set.

The calculator starts from world output of $118.35 trillion in 2025 and grows it at a constant real rate. Losses begin at the chosen share of output in 2025 and rise in a straight line to the chosen share in 2100. The chart shows the annual loss in trillions of 2025 dollars and the cumulative bill; the table splits it into three 25-year periods and sets the results beside a working range of estimates previously circulated in this series, so they can be checked against an explicit set of assumptions.

Two numbers are reported: the undiscounted total, which is what the century loses in 2025 dollars, and the present value, which weights later losses less. Both are legitimate. The undiscounted figure answers "how much output will the world not have"; the present value answers "how much is it worth spending today to avoid it". At the default settings the century produces about $26 quadrillion of output, and the central path loses about 8 per cent of it.

On the central path, losses rise from about $4.7 trillion in 2025 to about $75 trillion a year by 2100, because both the share and the economy grow. The bill is $205 trillion in 2025–2049, $529 trillion in 2050–2074 and $1.33 quadrillion in 2075–2100: almost two-thirds of the century's losses fall in its last 26 years. The total is about $2.1 quadrillion, or $781 trillion in present value at a 2 per cent discount rate and $514 trillion at 3 per cent.

The working range in the table — $100–300 trillion, $200–700 trillion and $300 trillion to over $2 quadrillion for the three periods, $600 trillion to over $3 quadrillion in total — falls inside the calculator's low-to-high band. A total of $600 trillion corresponds to losing about 2.3 per cent of output every year; $3 quadrillion to about 11.5 per cent. In other words, the range is a statement about the share of output lost, and the calculator makes that statement explicit.

Growth matters almost as much as the loss share. At 1.5 per cent real growth the central path costs $1.25 quadrillion; at 3 per cent, $2.7 quadrillion. Richer futures have more to lose. The quadrillion-dollar figures are therefore not exaggerations but the ordinary consequence of applying single-digit percentage losses to the output of a growing world economy over 75 years — which is also why preventing even a fraction of them is so valuable.

5. What Prevention Buys

Spending that avoids part of the loss pays for itself many times over a century. The question is not whether, but how much and how soon.

The calculator below adds prevention to the century bill. Each year the world spends a chosen share of output on prevention — clean energy, adaptation, pollution control, public health, conflict prevention — and in return avoids a chosen share of losses, phased in over twenty years because prevention takes time to build. The defaults are deliberately cautious: 1.5 per cent of output, avoiding 40 per cent of the central path's losses.

The evidence on returns is strong. The Global Commission on Adaptation estimated that investing $1.8 trillion in adaptation over a decade would yield $7.1 trillion in net benefits, with benefit-cost ratios of 2 to 10. International adaptation finance, however, reached only $26 billion in 2023, against needs the UN Environment Programme puts at $310 to $365 billion a year — a gap of twelve to fourteen times.

At the defaults, prevention avoids about $800 trillion of losses over the century for about $390 trillion of spending — roughly $2 back for every $1, and a net saving of about $400 trillion. In present value at 2 per cent the return is about 1.8 to 1. These are cautious numbers: the published benefit-cost ratios for adaptation, early warning and resilient infrastructure run from 2 to more than 10, and the calculator counts none of the co-benefits of cleaner air, healthier populations or more stable economies.

Timing matters as much as scale. Because losses grow with the economy and with the share lost, prevention that starts now avoids losses that would otherwise compound; prevention delayed to mid-century faces a larger stock of damage and a shorter period in which to recover its cost. The twenty-year ramp in the calculator is a reminder that most of what will protect the world economy in 2050 — grids, buildings, water systems, institutions — has to be built in the next two decades.

The ledger also points to where the money is. The largest current entries — pollution, violence and the climate liability of emissions — are the ones most amenable to prevention: cleaner energy removes most of the pollution cost and much of the climate liability, and conflict prevention costs a tiny fraction of what violence costs. The polycrisis bill is not a fixed charge. It is the price of the prevention the world has not yet bought.

6. Method

How the ledger and calculators work, and the limits of both.

World output is the World Bank's estimate for 2025, $118.35 trillion at current prices. Ledger entries are published estimates in their own years and currencies, listed with their sources; the economic impact of violence is converted from purchasing-power terms by applying its non-military share of gross world product (55 per cent of 11.6 per cent, as published by the Institute for Economics and Peace) to 2025 world output. The climate liability multiplies the Global Carbon Budget's 2025 total CO2 emissions, 42.2 billion tonnes, by the US EPA's 2023 social cost of carbon of $190 a tonne at a 2 per cent discount rate. Ledger totals are shares of 2025 output and are not adjusted for overlap.

The century calculator grows output at a constant real rate from 2025 and applies a loss share that rises linearly from its 2025 to its 2100 value; periods are 2025–2049, 2050–2074 and 2075–2100. The present value discounts each year's loss at the chosen rate to 2025. The prevention calculator spends a constant share of output each year and avoids a share of each year's losses that ramps up linearly over twenty years. All values are in 2025 dollars at market exchange rates. The working range in the period table is an earlier H Heuristics synthesis, shown for comparison.

The calculators omit growth effects (losses are treated as levels, not as reductions in the growth rate), tipping points and catastrophic tail risks, all of which would raise the bill, and they treat losses and prevention as smooth when both are lumpy. They are tools for making assumptions explicit, not forecasts. Related analysis in this series: the economics of prevention, the Polycrisis Apocalypse and resilience in an age of polycrisis.
